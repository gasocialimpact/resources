Data from the 2025 annual Council on Foundations-Commonfund Study of Investment of Endowments for Private and Community Foundations (CCSF; the "Study") shows an increase in private foundations and large public charities and nonprofit endowments that are contracting with outsourced investment management firms and Outsourced Chief Investment Officer (OCIO) service arrangements. The term OCIO refers to the practice whereby an external, third-party investment management firm assumes part or all of an organization's investment function.

Over time, Study data show that foundations across size and type cohorts have increased the share of investment management that is outsourced. In 2018, the first year this question was surveyed, 24% of respondents used an OCIO and those foundations outsourced 75% of their investments, on average. Fast forward to 2024: 38% of private foundation respondents worked with an OCIO, and those that did outsourced 93% of investments, on average. The 31% of community foundations that used an OCIO in 2018, and 46% in 2024, followed a similar trajectory to private foundations in terms of the growth of the share of investments outsourced. CCSF research team

Principally, the appeal of an outsourced investment management arrangement is that it allows fiduciaries and staff leaders to delegate many time-intensive day-to-day portfolio management activities and leverage financial expertise that may not be represented internally. While there are different outsourced investment management arrangements, often with different levels of outsourced discretionary investment decision-making, most foundations retain a high degree of fiduciary responsibility and control over the strategic asset allocation. Even with an OCIO arrangement, foundations practice good governance by articulating clear governance and operational procedures with outsourced investment service providers, routinely reviewing their IPS and asset allocation, and more.

Successful partnerships between foundations and outsourced investment managers require a high degree of trust and understanding, which often deepens over time and can produce "sticky," long-standing relationships. Foundations and institutional impact investors start a financial advisor search when their mission mandates change, their current advisor lacks impact expertise, or their governance model comes under review. Even when "all is well," foundations should regularly evaluate whether the outsourced arrangement is serving mission and business goals, which can lead to an OCIO search process to compare current versus prospective partners' financial performance, fee structure and annual expenses, service offerings, investment competencies, and culture and communication approaches. For many foundations, curiosity about or new internal commitments to socially responsible, values-aligned, and impact investing can surface misalignment with an existing OCIO provider and accelerate a search process and RFP issuance.

Identifying a credible, mission- and values-aligned investment manager can be a game-changer, but it is not a "silver bullet." Yes, the right OCIO can ease or accelerate values-aligned or impact investing adoption, but it can also help foundations reflect on internal alignment, impact investing readiness, and clarity of intentions. In other words, foundations should be prepared to work alongside and co-create with their outsourced investment managers to ensure that mission and financial goals are incorporated into portfolio construction and management mandates.

If you are still developing your impact investing framework, investment beliefs, or board alignment around mission-aligned investing, you may benefit more from strategy consulting first. If you have clarity on your approach and need to find the right advisor to execute it, then a search consultant or an internally managed search is the appropriate next step.

What is a Strategy Development Consultant?

A strategy development consultant helps you build internal capacity, design your overall impact investing strategy (including allocation targets across PRIs, MRIs, and ESG), develop measurement frameworks, and update governance documents. This typically happens before a foundation is ready to initiate an OCIO search, and may be performed by a non-advisory, non-investment-credentialed consultant.

Readiness Reflections

Before hiring a search consultant or issuing an RFP, honestly assess whether you are ready for an advisor search. As part of its 2025 investment evaluation process, the Dobbs Foundation recognized they needed to "build a shared understanding of impact investing by strengthening internal knowledge and capacity," "design an impact investing strategy," and "leverage the impact investing strategy to make appropriate updates to key governance documents" before they could effectively select and work with an investment advisor. If you are not confident in these foundational elements, rushing into an advisor search may lead to poor fit or unrealistic expectations.

Considering the Benefits & Drawbacks of an OCIO Search Consultant

A consultant facilitates the RFP process to select an investment advisor who will implement your impact investing approach. Typical fees run $25,000 to $75,000 depending on your asset size and the scope of the engagement.

The Benefits

  • Specialized expertise. Consultants in this space know the current landscape of impact-oriented advisors, understand emerging practices in mission-aligned investing, and can identify which firms have real depth versus marketing language. They stay current on fee structures, service models, and which advisors work well with foundations of your size and complexity. The Dobbs Foundation specifically sought consultants with "demonstrated expertise in advising private foundations on integrated grantmaking + investment strategies" and "designing PRIs, MRIs, and thematic funds across health, education, and environment," recognizing that generalist consultants would not have the necessary specialization.
  • Process management. Running an RFP involves considerable coordination: developing the question set, managing the timeline, ensuring comparability across responses, facilitating evaluation criteria, and organizing finalist presentations. A consultant handles these logistics and keeps the process moving while your team focuses on strategic decisions rather than administrative details.
  • Objective evaluation framework. An experienced consultant brings structured evaluation methods, helps you avoid common pitfalls in advisor selection, and can pressure-test your assumptions about what you actually need. They also provide a buffer that allows for candid assessment of proposals without the relationship dynamics that can arise when foundation staff interacts directly with potential advisors. The Dobbs RFP asks consultants directly: "What do foundations 'get wrong' at the stage the Foundation is currently at? How does your proposed approach address those common pitfalls?" This question acknowledges that outside expertise can help navigate known trouble spots.
  • Market intelligence. Beyond the immediate search, a good consultant will help you understand where your foundation sits relative to peers, what is reasonable to expect in terms of fees and services, and how your impact investing goals compare to what is currently achievable in the market. Local context matters here. A consultant familiar with Georgia's impact investing ecosystem, including organizations like the Georgia Social Impact Collaborative that the Dobbs Foundation has supported, can identify relevant regional opportunities and partnerships.

The Drawbacks

  • Cost. Search consultants typically charge between $25,000 and $75,000 depending on scope and your asset size. For a smaller foundation, this represents a meaningful expense that could otherwise support grantmaking or be invested.
  • Process ownership. When you manage the search internally, your team develops deeper knowledge of the investment advisor landscape and builds relationships that extend beyond the search. You also maintain more direct control over timing, emphasis, and decision criteria. The Dobbs approach of first building internal capacity through their strategy engagement reflects this principle. They wanted to ensure foundation staff and board developed genuine expertise rather than outsourcing the thinking entirely.
  • Available resources. Strong RFP templates already exist. Organizations like Mission Investors Exchange, the Jessie Smith Noyes Foundation, Impact Finance Center, and now the Dobbs Foundation have published their materials specifically so other foundations can learn from their experience. With these templates and a curated list of advisors to solicit, you have the core materials needed to run a credible process.
  • Foundation-specific knowledge. Your team understands your board culture, risk tolerance, grantmaking strategy, and organizational capacity better than any outside consultant will, even after a thorough discovery process. Some foundations find that external consultants, despite their best efforts, miss nuances that matter to final decision-making. The Dobbs RFP evaluation criteria weights "Fit & Understanding" at 35%, the highest of any category, recognizing that alignment with "the Foundation's needs, impact investing goals, culture, & values" is paramount.

If You Hire a Search Consultant

If your foundation chooses to hire a search consultant, establish clear working agreements upfront.

  • Role boundaries. The consultant should facilitate the process and provide expertise, but foundation staff and board make the actual decisions. Be explicit about where the consultant advises versus decides.
  • Communication protocols. How often will you meet? Who is the primary foundation contact? How does the consultant interact with board members versus staff? What approvals does the consultant need before communicating with prospective advisors? It might also be beneficial to request that consultants identify "the stakeholders involved" in their technical approach, ensuring clarity on who participates in what.
  • Confidentiality, conflicts, & fee transparency. Ensure the consultant does not have undisclosed relationships with firms being considered and agrees to keep foundation information confidential. Understand how the consultant is compensated, typically a flat fee from you, not contingent on which advisor is selected. Request details on the expected time and effort, as well as the accompanying deliverables that will be produced throughout the project work plan. This level of detail helps you understand what you are paying for and makes it easier to manage scope if project needs change.
  • Deliverables. Specify what you expect: RFP drafting, distribution list development, response analysis, evaluation framework, finalist presentation facilitation, reference checking, and a final recommendation memo.
  • Knowledge transfer. If you hire a consultant, ask them to document the process and rationale, so your team builds institutional knowledge for future searches or investment policy updates.

Hedging for Success: Good Groundwork that Precedes an OCIO Search

Whether you hire a consultant or manage this internally, the foundation needs to have clarity on several foundational questions before issuing an RFP.

  • Investment beliefs and policy framework. The AJL Foundation example shows how important it is to articulate your investment beliefs upfront. You need working answers, even if not finalized, to questions like: What is your theory about the relationship between impact investing and financial returns? What role should the endowment play in advancing mission? How do you think about tradeoffs between impact depth and portfolio diversification? What balance are you seeking between PRIs, MRIs, and ESG investments? These beliefs shape which advisors are the right fit. The Dobbs Foundation's three strategic focus areas (education, health services, environment) are well-defined, but they recognized the need for consulting support to determine "the right balance between PRIs, MRIs, and ESG investments" and to model "multiple impact investing portfolio allocation scenarios." If you have not worked through these allocation questions yet, you will struggle to evaluate advisor proposals meaningfully.
  • Current portfolio snapshot. Document your existing asset allocation, current advisor relationships (if any), fee structure, and performance. Prospective advisors need to understand what they are inheriting or changing. This also helps you identify specific pain points you want a new advisor to address. The Dobbs Foundation's current state (45% in ESG-aligned mutual funds, several completed PRIs) provides a baseline that any new advisor would build from.
  • Organizational capacity. Be realistic about how much staff and board time you can dedicate to investment oversight. Some advisor models require quarterly meetings, detailed reporting review, and active decision-making on manager selection. Others offer more turnkey approaches. Knowing your capacity helps you evaluate service models appropriately. The Dobbs Foundation explicitly plans to develop "a multi-year staff and board workplan for identifying and assessing potential appropriate investment managers or vehicles" and an "ongoing learning agenda that covers board & committee education," recognizing that building internal capacity is essential for effective advisor partnership.
  • Impact investing parameters. Get specific about what impact investing means for your foundation. The Jessie Smith Noyes example asked advisors to respond to field-level questions about social justice investing. The Dobbs Foundation defined clear parameters around their three program areas and geographic focus on Georgia. You should be able to articulate, even in draft form, which issues matter most, whether you are interested in screening strategies versus proactive impact investments, your geographic focus if any, and how you want to measure and report on impact outcomes. The Dobbs RFP asks consultants to help them "define impact goals and related tracking metrics." If you need this level of support, you are probably not ready for an advisor search yet. Advisors can help refine your impact measurement approach, but they should not be defining your impact goals. That is core to your mission and theory of change.
  • Budget and asset projections. Advisors structure fees differently. Share your current asset level, projected growth or distributions, and any planned large inflows or outflows to help prospective consultants scope the engagement appropriately. Also establish your budget range for advisory fees so you can evaluate proposals against realistic constraints.
  • Decision-making structure. Clarify who evaluates proposals, who conducts finalist interviews, and who makes the final selection. Understanding whether this is a board decision, an investment committee decision, or a staff recommendation with board approval shapes how you structure the process timeline. For example, in its 2025 RFP, the Dobbs Foundation noted that their board includes "Dobbs family members and selected community leaders" who transitioned to "a more formalized governance structure, including written policies, board orientation, and regular CEO oversight" in the early 2000s. This governance context matters when designing the search process.
  • Field-building intentions. Consider whether your foundation wants to contribute to ecosystem development or field-building beyond your own portfolio. There are plenty of foundations across the country and here in Georgia, take the Dobbs Foundation and The Sapelo Foundation, that see their impact investing work as contributing to field-building and peer practice-sharing. If you share this orientation, you might seek an advisor who engages in thought leadership, collaborates with other regional foundations, or helps develop local investment opportunities. If you are primarily focused on portfolio optimization, that is also a valid choice and should shape your advisor criteria.

For foundations who need to work with a strategy development consultant before issuing an OCIO search RFP, it is important to bring an open mind about where the right thought-partnership might come from. The impact investing consultancy and advisory field is replete with excellent resources, whether they be individual consultants, small-scale firms, specialized practice areas within larger asset management firms, or nonprofit field-builders. There is no right or wrong place to source the strategic support needed to guide future impact investing efforts, but foundations should be diligent, realistic, and scrutinizing to ensure the best results.

The impact investing strategy development process is separate and apart from portfolio construction and management services. Foundations need to have confidence that strategy development consultants will provide impartial guidance when setting impact investing policies and portfolio parameters. As such, there can be some angst when considering responses from investment advisors or asset managers who may have long-term OCIO ambitions. Healthy skepticism is good, but a fair counter-balancing argument acknowledges the potential value-added perspective that a professional investment advisory or asset management firm could bring. Some of the strongest strategy consultants have implementation arms precisely because they understand practical execution. What matters is whether their strategy work is genuinely independent and centered around the client's goals and aspirations. If an "all-in" or "100% mission-aligned portfolio" is something a foundation is striving for, that will inevitably require work with a professional investment advisor to develop a new portfolio-level strategy, and the best people to do that work with are investment managers, given that they:

  • Understand the realistic return expectations and market landscape;
  • May have better networks and deal flow insights;
  • Have deep practical knowledge of what works in implementation; and
  • Could accelerate total portfolio alignment after the strategy is complete.

What is most important is to distinguish between firms where asset management capability is actually valuable (deep implementation knowledge, realistic about what works, could accelerate portfolio alignment) versus problematic (proposal reads like lead generation, cannot demonstrate independence, predetermined recommendations). The strategy development RFP, follow-up interview questions, and reference checks can surface these issues, but some of the red flags to watch for include:

  • A proposal that reads like lead generation for ongoing services;
  • Inability to provide references for strategy-only engagements;
  • Predetermined recommendations that seem designed to drive advisory fees; and
  • Cannot articulate a clear separation between strategy development and "product shopping."

Tailoring & Targeting the RFP to Reach Ideal Respondents

Today's OCIO landscape has consolidated into three primary business models, each offering distinct advantages depending on an institutional investor's size, needs, and governance structure.

  • Asset managers and bank divisions. OCIO divisions of banks and asset managers typically offer a broad suite of financial services beyond investment management, often providing access to proprietary products or solutions.
  • Consulting firm extensions. Many traditional investment consultants have evolved to offer OCIO services alongside their legacy advisory businesses.
  • Boutiques. Boutique OCIOs are purpose-built to deliver Outsourced Chief Investment Officer services. They typically focus on discretionary investment management for a select number of institutional clients.

Any of the firm structures listed above could be considered, and a well-designed RFP will surface differences and similarities among firms. More importantly, it will ensure that respondents demonstrate their ability to meet non-negotiable expectations and competencies. Looking across published examples, successful RFPs generally include the following components.

  • Foundation background. Your mission, grantmaking focus, organizational structure, asset size, and any relevant history with impact investing or previous advisor relationships. The Dobbs RFP provides a comprehensive overview including their history since 1959, governance transition in the early 2000s, current strategic focus areas, and "key preceding impact investing activities" dating to 2008. This context helps respondents understand what you have learned, what you have tried, and what gaps remain.
  • Scope of work. Be explicit about what services you expect. The MRI example breaks this into initial asset allocation and advisory, ongoing monitoring and reporting, and specific expectations around mission-related investing support. The Dobbs RFP articulates three clear "project objectives" with specific activities under each, giving respondents a detailed picture of expected deliverables. This specificity helps you get comparable proposals rather than generic capabilities statements.
  • Investment beliefs and values. As the AJL example demonstrates, sharing your investment beliefs upfront allows advisors to respond specifically to your framework rather than providing generic answers. The Dobbs Foundation embeds their values throughout the RFP, from their mission statement emphasizing "educational opportunities, improving access to health services, and promoting environmental stewardship" to their specific program priorities and their history with GSIC and peer learning.
  • Detailed questions. Question sets across published examples cover the essential territory: firm profile and stability, team experience and staffing, investment philosophy and process, impact investing capabilities and track record, reporting and technology, fees and fee structure, and references. The Dobbs RFP includes both narrative proposal requirements and "supplemental questions" that get at nuanced issues like "What do foundations 'get wrong' at the stage the Foundation is currently at?" and "What are the most important success factors that help a foundation of our size do the greatest amount of good with our financial assets?" These questions invite consultants to share genuine expertise rather than just describe their services. You can adapt these to emphasize what matters most to your foundation. The Dobbs approach of limiting narrative proposals to eight pages and supplemental responses to two pages, while allowing unlimited appendices of example tools, strikes a good balance between comparability and allowing firms to showcase relevant work.
  • Evaluation criteria. Tell respondents how you will evaluate proposals. The AJL RFP lists six criteria (organization, experience and staffing, services, impact, fees, references) and explicitly notes these are not ranked. The Dobbs RFP goes further by assigning specific weights: Fit & Understanding (35%), Expertise & Experience (30%), Quality of Deliverables (25%), and Cost & Timeline (10%). This transparency helps firms understand what to emphasize and also forces your team to think clearly about what matters most.
  • Process and timeline. Clear deadlines, contact protocols, and a timeline for finalist selection and presentations. The Jessie Smith Noyes example explicitly instructs respondents not to contact the foundation directly, which helps maintain process integrity. The Dobbs RFP provides a detailed timeline table showing RFP issuance, proposal deadline, interview dates, and final notice, as well as desired project start and conclusion timeframes.
  • Practical details. Submission format (electronic versus hard copy), page limits or guidance, and specific materials to include, such as sample reports, fee schedules, or standard contract language. The Dobbs RFP included specific directions: "Please include the Subject Line: 'RFP Response – Impact Investing Strategy – Firm (or Individual) Name' and attach three separate PDFs in your submission: Narrative proposal, Responses to the Foundation's supplementary questions, Appendices with example tools/deliverables." This level of detail makes evaluation much easier.

Setting Realistic Expectations: Common Process Elements & Timing

Based on published examples, a typical search process includes these phases.

  1. Preparation (4 to 6 weeks). Finalize investment beliefs, develop RFP questions, identify firms to solicit, establish evaluation criteria, and confirm internal timeline and roles. The Dobbs Foundation timeline shows the importance of being realistic here. They allocated several months for their strategy development work before they would even be ready to search for advisors.
  2. RFP issuance and response (4 to 6 weeks). Distribute the RFP to selected firms, hold a deadline for questions, and allow adequate time for thoughtful responses. The AJL timeline gave firms about 3.5 weeks to respond, which is reasonable for a comprehensive RFP. The Dobbs Foundation uses a question deadline one week before the proposal deadline, giving them time to clarify issues for all respondents.
  3. Initial evaluation (2 to 3 weeks). Review responses against your criteria, eliminate firms that do not meet requirements, and identify three to five finalists for deeper consideration.
  4. Finalist presentations (2 to 4 weeks). Invite finalists to present in person or virtually. The Jessie Smith Noyes process selected up to five firms for a detailed RFP and meetings. The Dobbs Foundation plans specific interview dates in their timeline. Schedule these presentations close together so you can compare approaches while they are fresh.
  5. Final evaluation and selection (2 to 3 weeks). Check references, negotiate fee terms if needed, make the final selection, and notify all participants. The Dobbs RFP requires "three client references that the Foundation may contact" and asks consultants to "include narrative descriptions of up to four relevant project examples," providing substantial material for due diligence.
  6. Transition and onboarding (variable). Work with the selected advisor on transition planning, documentation, and initial portfolio review.

Total timeline typically runs 4 to 6 months from RFP preparation through contract signing. You can compress this if needed, but rushing the process often means missing important considerations or limiting the pool of qualified respondents.
